Choose a size for your glass jar

Each design includes small, medium and large wraps with coordinating printable specimen jar labels. The smallest wrap measures about 11 × 4.3 inches (28 × 11 cm), a handy size for jam or peanut butter jars. For mason canning jars, measure the straight section below the shoulder. The largest wrap suits a taller mason jar up to about 7.5 inches (19 cm) high. If you have a huge mason jar, run a strip of paper around the inside as well. The height alone won’t tell you whether the ends will meet. Once you’ve picked a spot for your jar, turn the seam toward the back. A small gap between the ends is usually fine when you’re looking at the jar from the front.

A place for your jar of eyes

For a Wunderkammer cabinet of curiosities, I like the eyes at eye level. Walk past the shelf and a different one seems to catch your gaze. On a narrow gothic wall shelf, a single jar has room to stand out. In a larger cabinet of curiosities and oddities, try the brain and mealworm jars alongside it, with their labels turned forward like a row of museum specimens.

If your collection already includes a taxidermy display, the printed specimens can sit beside it. I’d tuck a little gothic ephemera behind the glass jars and let the matching black-and-white labels tie the shelf together. You can also use the witchy bottle labels on spare bottles to carry the Old Salem apothecary theme a little further.

For a miniature spider terrarium, put a little sand in a jar, add a few twigs with dry leaves still attached, and stretch wisps of off-white thread between them for cobwebs. For another specimen, fill a little bottle with imitation bones or real bones that have been thoroughly cleaned and dried. A matching label makes either one look at home beside the jar of eyes.

Try the smallest print in a jam jar on the party table. For an eyeballs in jar DIY night, lay out the three designs and let everyone choose a specimen. Once the evening is over, the finished jars can go straight back on your oddities shelf.
